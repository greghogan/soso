Where does the energy go?

A collector absorbs energy, shares it with the water and its own materials, and exchanges heat with the room. Change one assumption at a time to explore the tradeoff. These curves are calculated examples, not measured build results or tournament predictions.

What does this model assume?

Water, cup and the thermally connected part of the device have one uniform temperature. Absorbed power and the heat-loss coefficient stay constant. There is no evaporation, boiling, changing lamp output, thermal contact delay, or temperature difference within the cup. Real devices break these assumptions, so this is a way to reason about trends, not a substitute for repeated tests. Official procedures prohibit swirling, shaking or stirring before measurement. A stratified cup can give a local reading different from this uniform-temperature model; reproduce the supervisor’s measurement protocol in practice.

A higher electrical bulb rating does not directly give the absorbed power. Neither does a simple inverse-square calculation: the lamp has an extended bulb and a reflector. The geometry explorer intentionally does not produce a temperature estimate.

How is the curve calculated?

At each temperature, net heating power = absorbed power − loss coefficient × (temperature − room temperature). Total heat capacity = 418 J/°C for the water + the selected extra heat capacity. Dividing net heating power by heat capacity gives the instantaneous warming rate.

The software uses the exact exponential solution of this simple constant-parameter model. The model mathematics is optional background; the event does not require calculus. With zero heat loss, the temperature rises steadily by absorbed energy divided by total heat capacity.

Try an experiment with the model
  1. Keep the default curve, then raise only extra heat capacity. Why does warming slow?
  2. Reset. Keep a curve, then lower only the heat-loss coefficient. Is the difference larger early or late?
  3. Set absorbed power to zero and start below room temperature. Where does the warming energy come from?
  4. Design a real one-variable experiment to check one of these trends. Measure after the same cup-removal delay each time.
